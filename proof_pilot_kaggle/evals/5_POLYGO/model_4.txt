**Solution.**  
Let  

\[
P(x)=x^{7}+a_{6}x^{6}+a_{5}x^{5}+a_{4}x^{4}+a_{3}x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{0}
\qquad (a_i\in\mathbb Z)
\]

be a *good* polynomial.
For \(i=0,\dots ,6\) put  

\[
c_i:=|a_i|\;(>0),\qquad c_7:=|a_7|=1 .
\]

All the numbers \(c_i\) are odd, satisfy \(c_i<2025^{2}\) and, by definition of a good
polynomial,

\[
P(c_i)=0\qquad (i=0,\dots ,7). \tag{1}
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  Preliminary observations

*Because the leading coefficient is \(1\), evaluating at \(x=1\) gives*

\[
P(1)=1+a_6+\dots +a_0=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 
a_6+\dots +a_0=-1 .\tag{2}
\]

Since each \(a_i\) is odd, (2) implies that among the seven
numbers \(a_0,\dots ,a_6\) exactly three are \(+1\) and four are \(-1\).  
Consequently every \(c_i\) equals either \(1\) or \(|a_i|>1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  The largest absolute coefficient

Let  

\[
r:=\max\{c_0,c_1,\dots ,c_6\}.
\]

If every \(c_i=1\) we shall deal with this case in §4.
Hence assume from now on that \(r>1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
#### Lemma 2.1  
\(c_0=r\) and \(c_0\) is the absolute value of the product of all
roots of \(P\).

*Proof.*  
Write the factorisation of \(P\) by its roots \(\alpha_1,\dots ,\alpha_7\) :

\[
P(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{7}(x-\alpha_j),\qquad
\alpha_j\in\mathbb C .
\]

The constant term is
\[
a_0=(-1)^{7}\prod_{j=1}^{7}\alpha_j=-\prod_{j=1}^{7}\alpha_j .
\]
Hence  

\[
c_0=|a_0|=\Bigl|\prod_{j=1}^{7}\alpha_j\Bigr| .\tag{3}
\]

All numbers \(c_i\) are roots of \(P\) by (1), thus
\(1\) (the absolute value of the leading coefficient) is a root.
Consequently \(\prod_{j=1}^{7}\alpha_j\) is a product of numbers each
of which has absolute value at least \(1\); therefore the
right‑hand side of (3) is at least the largest of the numbers
\(|\alpha_j|\).  By (1) every \(|\alpha_j|\) belongs to the set
\(\{c_0,\dots ,c_6\}\); hence \(\max_j |\alpha_j|=r\).
Thus \(c_0\ge r\).
On the other hand \(c_0\) itself belongs to the set
\(\{c_0,\dots ,c_6\}\); therefore \(c_0\le r\).
Consequently \(c_0=r\).  ∎



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  Reduction to a quadratic factor

Because of \(P(1)=0\) (see (2)), the linear factor \((x-1)\) divides
\(P\).  By Lemma 2.1 the integer \(r=c_0\) is a root of \(P\);
hence \((x-r)\) also divides \(P\).
Write

\[
P(x)=(x-1)(x-r)Q(x) ,
\tag{4}
\]

where \(Q\) is monic of degree \(5\) and has integer coefficients.
Put  

\[
Q(x)=x^{5}+b_{4}x^{4}+b_{3}x^{3}+b_{2}x^{2}+b_{1}x+b_{0},
\qquad b_j\in\mathbb Z .
\]

Expanding (4) and comparing coefficients with the generic form of
\(P\) yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
a_{6}&=b_{4}-(1+r),\\[2mm]
a_{5}&=b_{3}-(1+r)b_{4}+r,\\
a_{4}&=b_{2}-(1+r)b_{3}+rb_{4},\\
a_{3}&=b_{1}-(1+r)b_{2}+rb_{3},\\
a_{2}&=b_{0}-(1+r)b_{1}+rb_{2},\\
a_{1}&=-(1+r)b_{0}+rb_{1},\\
a_{0}&=r\,b_{0}.
\end{aligned}\tag{5}
\]

Recall that \(c_i=|a_i|\) equals either \(1\) or \(r\); the latter is
the maximal value.
From the last line of (5),

\[
c_0=|a_{0}|=|r\,b_{0}| .
\]

Since \(c_0=r>1\), the only possibility is  

\[
|b_{0}|=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad c_0=r.\tag{6}
\]

Let us write \(b_{0}= \varepsilon _0\) with \(\varepsilon _0\in\{\pm1\}\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
#### Lemma 3.1  
\(c_{1}=1\) and \(b_{1}=-\varepsilon _0\).

*Proof.*  
From (5) and (6),

\[
a_{1}=-(1+r)\varepsilon _0+r b_{1},
\qquad 
c_{1}=|a_{1}|\le r .
\]

If \(c_{1}=r\) then  

\[
r=|-(1+r)\varepsilon _0+r b_{1}|
   =|r(b_{1}-\varepsilon _0)-\varepsilon _0| .
\]

Dividing by \(r\) gives \(|b_{1}-\varepsilon _0-\varepsilon _0/r|=1\).
Because \(r>1\) the fraction \(\varepsilon _0/r\) is not integral,
hence the equality is impossible.  Thus \(c_{1}\neq r\); consequently
\(c_{1}=1\).
Now \( |-(1+r)\varepsilon _0+r b_{1}| =1\) implies
\(r b_{1} = (1+r)\varepsilon _0\pm1\).
Since \(r\) does not divide the integer \(\pm1\) (again because \(r>1\)),
the only way to obtain an integer \(b_{1}\) is  

\[
b_{1} = -\varepsilon _0 .
\tag{7}
\]

∎



--------------------------------------------------------------------
#### Lemma 3.2  
No odd integer \(r>1\) satisfies the remaining equations (5).

*Proof.*  
Insert \(b_{0}=\varepsilon _0\) and \(b_{1}=-\varepsilon _0\) from (6)–(7)
into the expression for \(a_{2}\) in (5):

\[
a_{2}= \varepsilon _0-(1+r)(-\varepsilon _0)+r b_{2}
      = r b_{2}+\varepsilon _0(r+2).   \tag{8}
\]

Hence  

\[
c_{2}=|a_{2}|=|r b_{2}+ \varepsilon _0(r+2)|
          =|r(b_{2}+\varepsilon _0)+2\varepsilon _0| .\tag{9}
\]

Because the absolute values of the coefficients may only be
\(1\) or \(r\), we must have \(c_{2}\in\{1,r\}\).

*Case \(c_{2}=1\).*  
Then \(|r(b_{2}+\varepsilon _0)+2\varepsilon _0|=1\).
The term \(r|b_{2}+\varepsilon _0|\) is a multiple of \(r\ge3\)
and therefore its absolute value is at least \(3\) unless it is
zero.  If it is zero we obtain \(|2\varepsilon _0|=2\neq1\).
Hence this case is impossible.

*Case \(c_{2}=r\).*  
Now \(|r(b_{2}+\varepsilon _0)+2\varepsilon _0|=r\), i.e.  

\[
|\,b_{2}+\varepsilon _0+\frac{2\varepsilon _0}{r}\,|=1 .
\]

Since \(0<\bigl|\frac{2\varepsilon _0}{r}\bigr|<1\) (recall \(r\ge3\)),
the left‑hand side can be equal to \(1\) only when
\(b_{2}+\varepsilon _0=0\) and \(\frac{2\varepsilon _0}{r}=0\),
the latter being impossible for \(r>1\).
Thus this case also leads to a contradiction.

Both possibilities for \(c_{2}\) are impossible; therefore no odd
\(r>1\) can satisfy the system (5). ∎



--------------------------------------------------------------------
From Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 we deduce that the assumption \(r>1\) is
false. Consequently

\[
\boxed{c_0=c_1=\dots =c_6=1 } .
\tag{10}
\]

That is, **every coefficient of a good polynomial has absolute value
\(1\).**  Hence each coefficient is either \(+1\) or \(-1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4.  Determining all good polynomials

Because all \(|a_i|=1\), condition (1) reduces to the single requirement
that \(1\) be a root of \(P\); indeed \(P(1)=0\) is equivalent to  

\[
1+a_6+\dots +a_0=0 .
\tag{11}
\]

Let \(p\) be the number of coefficients equal to \(+1\) among
\(a_0,\dots ,a_6\); then the remaining \(7-p\) coefficients equal \(-1\).
Equation (11) becomes  

\[
1+(p-(7-p))=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2p-6=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p=3 .
\]

Thus exactly three of the seven coefficients \(a_{0},\dots ,a_{6}\) are
\(+1\) and the other four are \(-1\).  
Choosing the three positions of the \(+1\)’s determines the polynomial
uniquely; there are  

\[
\binom{7}{3}=35
\]

such choices.

Every polynomial obtained in this way is monic of degree \(7\),
has all coefficients odd and smaller than \(2025^{2}\),
and satisfies \(P(1)=0\); because each absolute coefficient equals
\(1\), condition (1) is fulfilled.  Hence all \(35\) polynomials are
good.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.  Conclusion

The only possible good polynomials are those whose coefficients are
\(\pm1\) with exactly three \(+1\)’s and four \(-1\)’s; there are  

\[
\boxed{35}
\]

such polynomials. ∎
