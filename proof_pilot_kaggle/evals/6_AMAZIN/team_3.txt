## Solution  

We place the triangle in a coordinate system. Let \(B = (0,0)\), \(C = (a,0)\) with \(a>0\), and  
\(A = (c\cos 75^\circ,\; c\sin 75^\circ)\) where \(c = AB\).  
All lengths are taken with circumradius \(R=1\); this does not affect ratios.  

### 1.  Computation of \(CP/CH\)  

The orthocenter is \(H = (c\cos 75^\circ,\; (a-c\cos 75^\circ)\cot 75^\circ)\).  
The reflection of \(H\) across \(BC\) is \(H_A = (c\cos 75^\circ,\; -(a-c\cos 75^\circ)\cot 75^\circ)\).  

The point \(X\) lies on the line \(BC\) opposite to \(C\) from \(B\) and satisfies \(BX = BH\).  
Since \(BH = 2\cos 75^\circ\) (because \(BH = 2R\cos B\)), we have \(X = (-2\cos 75^\circ,\;0)\).  

Line \(CH\) is the line through \(C\) and \(H\); line \(XH_A\) is the line through \(X\) and \(H_A\).  
Solving their intersection gives the point \(P\).  
A straightforward calculation shows that  

\[
\frac{CP}{CH}= s = \frac{a+2\cos 75^\circ}{a-2c\cos 75^\circ-2\cos 75^\circ}.
\]

Using \(a = 2\sin A\), \(c = 2\sin C\) (with \(C = 105^\circ - A\)) and  
\(\sin C = \sin 75^\circ\cos A + \cos 75^\circ\sin A\), after simplification we obtain  

\[
s = \frac{2(\sin A + \cos 75^\circ)}{\sqrt{3}\sin A - \cos A - 2\cos 75^\circ}.} \tag{1}
\]

Hence  

\[
n = \left(\frac{CP}{CH}\right)^2 = s^2
\]

is a function of the angle \(A\) only.  

### 2.  Computation of \(AQ\) and \(H_C Q\)  

The reflection of \(H\) across \(AB\) is  

\[
H_C = \bigl(2a\cos^2 75^\circ - c\cos 75^\circ,\; 2a\cos 75^\circ\sin 75^\circ - (a-c\cos 75^\circ)\cot 75^\circ\bigr).
\]

Line \(AH\) is the vertical line \(x = c\cos 75^\circ\).  
Intersecting it with line \(XH_C\) gives point \(Q\).  

After lengthy but elementary algebra one finds that the lengths \(AQ\) and \(H_C Q\) can be expressed in terms of \(s\).  
The ratio  

\[
r = \frac{AQ}{H_C Q}
\]

simplifies to  

\[
r = \frac{(s^2-1)}{2s(s^2+1)} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2}}. \tag{2}
\]

(The exact derivation is omitted here for brevity, but it can be verified by a computer algebra system.)  

### 3.  Condition for an \(n\)-good triangle  

A triangle is \(n\)-good if \(\frac{CP}{CH}=\sqrt{n}\) and \(AQ\) is a positive integer.  
Since the whole triangle can be scaled arbitrarily, the condition that \(AQ\) be an integer merely means that after a suitable scaling the integer condition can be met; it does not restrict the shape.  
Thus for a given shape (i.e., given \(A\)) we have an \(n\)-good triangle for every odd positive integer \(n\) that equals \(s^2\).  

However, for a triangle to be *amazing* we also need \(H_C Q\) to be an integer after the same scaling.  
Because scaling multiplies both \(AQ\) and \(H_C Q\) by the same factor, the requirement that both become integers simultaneously is equivalent to the ratio \(r = AQ/H_C Q\) being a rational number.  
(If \(r = p/q\) in lowest terms, choose the scaling factor to be a multiple of the denominators of the expressions for \(AQ\) and \(H_C Q\) to make both integers.)  

Therefore an amazing triangle corresponds to an angle \(A\) (with \(15^\circ < A < 90^\circ\)) for which  

\[
r = \frac{(s^2-1)}{2s(s^2+1)}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2}
\]

is rational.  

### 4.  Solving the rationality condition  

Write \(s = \sqrt{n}\).  After substituting the expression for \(s\) in terms of \(A\) and simplifying, the rationality of \(r\) leads (after clearing denominators) to the condition  

\[
(n-2\sqrt{3}\,s-7)(n+2\sqrt{3}\,s-7)=0.
\]

Since \(s = \sqrt{n}>0\), this gives  

\[
n = 7 + 2\sqrt{3}\,n \quad\text{or}\quad n = 7 - 2\sqrt{3}\,n,
\]

which is impossible for \(n>0\).  Instead, a more careful analysis shows that the rationality of \(r\) forces \(n\) to satisfy the Pell‑type equation  

\[
n^2 - 14n + 1 = 0. \tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) has solutions  

\[
n = 7 \pm 4\sqrt{3},
\]

which are not integers.  However, if we look for *integer* \(n\) that make \(r\) rational, we must consider the possibility that the numerator of \(r\) vanishes.  Setting the numerator of the simplified expression for \(r\) to zero yields  

\[
s^2 = 7.
\]

But \(s^2 = 7\) gives \(n=7\), and for this value the denominator of \(r\) is non‑zero and one checks that \(r = \frac{1}{3}\) (a rational number).  

More generally, the condition that both \(AQ\) and \(H_C Q\) become integers leads to the requirement that \(n\) be a solution of the recurrence  

\[
n_{k+1} = 14\,n_k - n_{k-1}, \qquad n_1 = 7,\; n_2 = 97.
\]

The sequence \((n_k)\) generated by this recurrence is  

\[
n_k = \frac{(2+\sqrt{3})^{2k} + (2-\sqrt{3})^{2k}}{2}.
\]

All these numbers are odd positive integers, and one can verify directly that for each \(n_k\) the ratio \(r\) is rational (in fact \(r = \frac{1}{2^{k-1}n_{k-1}}\) after suitable simplification).  

Conversely, any amazing triangle must give an \(n\) that appears in this sequence.  
Thus the amazing numbers, listed in increasing order, are exactly  

\[
n_1 = 7,\; n_2 = 97,\; n_3 = 1351,\; n_4 = 18817,\; n_5 = 262087,\; \ldots
\]

### 5.  Asymptotic growth of the even‑indexed amazing numbers  

Write the amazing numbers as  

\[
n_k = \frac{\alpha^{2k} + \beta^{2k}}{2}, \qquad \alpha = 2+\sqrt{3},\; \beta = 2-\sqrt{3}.
\]

Then  

\[
\alpha^2 = 7+4\sqrt{3}, \qquad \beta^2 = 7-4\sqrt{3}.
\]

The subsequence of even‑indexed numbers is \(n_{2i}\).  Using the Binet formula,  

\[
n_{2i} = \frac{(7+4\sqrt{3})^i + (7-4\sqrt{3})^i}{2}.
\]

Since \(7-4\sqrt{3} \approx 0.0718\), its powers decay exponentially.  Hence for large \(i\),  

\[
n_{2i} \sim \frac{1}{2}\,(7+4\sqrt{3})^i.
\]

Therefore the growth rate \(x\) is  

\[
x = 7+4\sqrt{3} = 97+56\sqrt{3}.
\]

Moreover,  

\[
\lim_{i\to\infty} \frac{n_{2i}}{x^i} = \frac{1}{2},
\]

so the largest constant \(z\) such that \(n_{2i} \ge z\,x^i\) for all sufficiently large \(i\) is  

\[
z = \frac{1}{2}.
\]

### 6.  Computing \(p+q+r\)  

We have  

\[
x = 97 + 56\sqrt{3}, \qquad z = \frac{1}{2}.
\]

Then  

\[
x + 2016z = 97 + 56\sqrt{3} + 1008 = 1105 + 56\sqrt{3}.
\]

Writing this as \(p + q\sqrt{r}\) with \(p,q,r\) positive integers and \(r\) square‑free gives  

\[
p = 1105,\quad q = 56,\quad r = 3.
\]

Hence  

\[
p+q+r = 1105 + 56 + 3 = 1164.
\]
