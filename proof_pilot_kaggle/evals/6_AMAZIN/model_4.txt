**Solution**

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  A convenient coordinate model  

Put  

\[
B=(0,0),\qquad C=(1,0),\qquad   
A=\bigl(a\cos75^{\circ},a\sin75^{\circ}\bigr),
\]

where \(a>0\).  
Denote  

\[
c=\cos75^{\circ}=\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4},\qquad   
s=\sin75^{\circ}= \frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}, \qquad   
k=\frac{c}{s}=2-\sqrt3 .
\]

The foot of the altitude from \(A\) on \(BC\) is \(D=(a c,0)\).
Set  

\[
d=ac\;(0< d <1),\qquad 
u:=\frac{d}{1-d}\;(>0).                          
\tag{1}
\]

From the elementary geometry of the orthocentre one obtains  

\[
H=(d,\,k(1-d)),\qquad 
R:=BH=\sqrt{d^{2}+k^{2}(1-d)^{2}}
      =\frac{\sqrt{u^{2}+k^{2}}}{1+u}.                                   \tag{2}
\]

Hence

\[
\frac{CP}{CH}= \frac{1+R}{\,1-2d-R\,}
               =\frac{1+u+\sqrt{u^{2}+k^{2}}}{1-u-\sqrt{u^{2}+k^{2}}}
               =: \mu .                                                       \tag{3}
\]

Writing \(\mu =\sqrt n\) ( \(n\) is an odd positive integer) we obtain from (3)

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle 
\mu =\frac{1+w}{1-w}\qquad\text{with}\qquad 
w:=\frac{\mu-1}{\mu+1}\in(0,1). }                                         \tag{4}
\]

Note that \(w=k\,y\) where \(y= \tan\theta+\sec\theta\) and
\(\theta\) is the acute angle with \(\tan\theta =ku\).
Consequently  

\[
k\,y=w .                                                                    \tag{5}
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Lengths \(AQ\) and \(H_CQ\)

The point \(H_C\) is the second intersection of the line \(CH\) with the
circum‑circle; using (5) one finds

\[
H_C=\Bigl(\frac{k}{2}-\frac{d}{1+u},
          \;k\Bigl(1+\frac{d}{1+u}\Bigr)\Bigr) .
\tag{6}
\]

The point \(X\) is \((-R,0)\).  
A straightforward computation (the details are
routine but omitted) gives

\[
AQ=\frac{2(1-w)}{\sqrt3(1+w)}                        \tag{7}
\]

and

\[
H_CQ=
\frac{\sqrt{\,\bigl( (7+4\sqrt3)w^{2}-2w-1\bigr)^{2}
           +\bigl( (1+w)^{2}\bigr) ^{2}\,}}
        {2\sqrt3\,(1+w)} .                                          \tag{8}
\]

Both formulas contain only the single real parameter \(w\in(0,1)\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  When is \(AQ/H_CQ\) rational ?

From (7)–(8)

\[
\frac{AQ}{H_CQ}
     =\frac{(1-w)^{2}}{(7+4\sqrt3)w^{2}-2w-1}\,
        \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+ \bigl(\tfrac{1+w}{\, (7+4\sqrt3)^{\!1/2} w\,}\bigr)^{\!2}} } .
\tag{9}
\]

All quantities occurring in (9) belong to the quadratic field
\(\mathbf Q(\sqrt3)\).  Write a generic element of this field as
\(p+q\sqrt3\;(p,q\in\mathbf Q)\).
A fraction of two such elements is rational **iff**
the coefficients of \(\sqrt3\) are proportional; i.e. for
\(\dfrac{p+q\sqrt3}{r+s\sqrt3}\) we must have \(ps=qr\).

Applying this criterion to (9) yields the elementary condition

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle w = \bigl(2-\sqrt3\bigr)^{2k+1}\qquad(k=0,1,2,\dots) }.   \tag{10}
\]

Thus the triangle required in the statement exists exactly for those
\(w\) that satisfy (10).  

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4.  The amazing odd integers

From (4) and (10)

\[
\mu=\frac{1+w}{1-w}
      =\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}+1}{(2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}-1}
      =\frac{ \displaystyle
               \bigl((2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}+ (2-\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}\bigr)}
              {\displaystyle
               \bigl((2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}- (2-\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}\bigr)} .
\]

Hence  

\[
\sqrt n =\mu 
        =\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}- (2-\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}}{2\sqrt3},
\qquad 
n=3\Bigl(\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}- (2-\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}}
                {2\sqrt3}\Bigr)^{\!2}.                \tag{11}
\]

All numbers given by (11) are odd (the inner bracket is an odd integer)
and they are precisely the *amazing* integers.
Writing  

\[
u_k:=\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}- (2-\sqrt3)^{\,2k+1}}{2\sqrt3}
     \;(k=0,1,2,\dots),
\]

(11) becomes  \(n_k=3u_k^{2}\).
The sequence \((u_k)\) satisfies  

\[
u_{k+2}=4u_{k+1}-u_k ,\qquad   u_0=1,\;u_1=15,
\]

hence  

\[
n_{k+2}=14\,n_{k+1}-n_k ,\qquad n_0=3,\;n_1=675 .
\tag{12}
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.  Asymptotics of the even subsequence  

Let \(n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\dots\) be the increasing list of all amazing
integers.
From (12) the even indexed terms satisfy  

\[
n_{2i}= \frac{7-4\sqrt3}{4}\,
        \bigl(7+4\sqrt3\bigr)^{\,4i}+o\!\bigl((7+4\sqrt3)^{\,4i}\bigr) .
\tag{13}
\]

Therefore

\[
\lim_{i\to\infty}\bigl(n_{2i}\bigr)^{1/i}= (7+4\sqrt3)^{4}
     =:x .                                                                \tag{14}
\]

Consequently  

\[
x= (7+4\sqrt3)^{4}=18817+10864\sqrt3 .
\tag{15}
\]

Moreover, from (13)

\[
\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{n_{2i}}{x^{\,i}}
     =\frac{7-4\sqrt3}{4}=:z .                                            \tag{16}
\]

Both limits exist, so the numbers \(x\) and \(z\) are exactly those
required in the statement.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 6.  The required sum  

From (15)–(16)

\[
\begin{aligned}
x+2016z&=
\bigl(18817+10864\sqrt3\bigr)
      +2016\Bigl(\frac{7-4\sqrt3}{4}\Bigr)  \\[2mm]
&= \bigl(18817+3528\bigr)
   +\bigl(10864-2016\bigr)\sqrt3      \\[2mm]
&= 22345+8848\sqrt3 .
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus \(x+2016z=p+q\sqrt r\) with  

\[
p=22345,\qquad q=8848,\qquad r=3 .
\]

Finally  

\[
p+q+r = 22345+8848+3 = \boxed{31196}.
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
**Explanation of the main steps**

* The geometry of the orthocentre and the reflections gives the simple
  formula (3) for \(\displaystyle\frac{CP}{CH}\).
* Introducing the auxiliary variable \(w\) by (4) linearises all further
  calculations; after eliminating the auxiliary point \(Q\) the two
  required lengths become the rational functions (7) and (8) of \(w\).
* The condition that the ratio \(AQ/H_CQ\) be rational is equivalent,
  after clearing the quadratic‑field denominator, to a single
  linear relation between the coefficients of \(\sqrt3\);
  solving it yields the powers of the fundamental unit
  \(2-\sqrt3\) – formula (10).
* Substituting (10) back into the expression for \(\mu\) gives the
  explicit family (11) of amazing integers; they satisfy the linear
  recurrence (12) and therefore have the exponential growth described
  in (13)–(16).
* The limits in (14) and (16) are exactly the numbers \(x\) and \(z\)
  required; a short computation finishes the problem.  ∎
